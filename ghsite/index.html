Engine One — creator growth collaborations.

We put the marketing budget into people instead of platform ad spend. Gift boxes, event collab packs, micro-influencer campaigns, docu-profiles — converting money into product, relationships and film that keeps working after the spend stops.

  • The goal is people who make content. Not a celebrity list. The people worth our time are already producing — running events, filming, building an audience with their own hands. Someone with 40,000 followers who films every week beats a name with two million who posts twice a year.
  • Instruments: the gift box (~$40–55) · the event collab pack (~$200) · production at $12 a reel · the targeted platform invite at zero cost.
  • Cadence: 15–25 names a month, drafted by Amechi, approved by Keys before a note is written.

Engine Two — the affiliate program.

Open to anyone. The job isn't to recruit carefully — it's to build a community of sellers, check in on them constantly, and hand them the tools to be good at it. People don't fail here for lack of motivation. They fail because nobody told them what to post or answered their message.

The rhythm is the product

  • A daily 10-minute check so nobody waits days for a reply or a code.
  • A Thursday call, every week — wins, one product story, the format that's converting, open floor.
  • A monthly challenge with a hashtag, a deadline and a prize.
  • A tier that moves the moment someone earns it — $400 in 30 days to Creator, $1,000 to Partner with a membership card in the mail.
  • Reciprocity: when an affiliate hosts something, we promote it in Zion. We don't just take their content — we fill their rooms.

Because there has never been a way to sign up. Every one of the 23 came through a personal conversation. No application page, no funnel, no automated welcome, no onboarding. The program has been a series of favours, not a system.

The doctrine governing every payment

Who we're talking to.

The audience skews to Black women, roughly 25–45, who are building something — she works, creates, runs something or is about to, takes her mornings seriously, and is tired of wellness that talks down to her.

That's the default reader for the newsletter, the default casting for photography, and the weighting on the creator list — roughly two-thirds women per tranche. Keys' own audience and the workout collaborations he brings cover the other side, through his brand rather than through this program.

The Handbook.

What every affiliate receives. Written to them, not about them — short, warm, and specific about where the money is.

The four things we ask every time

#The askWhy
1Post it as a collaborative post with @supermindThe highest-leverage thing they can do and it costs nothing. Lands on our feed and theirs at once, shares the engagement, puts them in front of our whole audience. We accept whatever is genuinely good — and good means it fits their page, not that it looks like an ad.
2Tag the brand, alwaysEven when it isn't a collab post. It's how we find their work to repost it — and we repost a creator every single day.
3Disclose"#ad", "commissionable link", or said on camera. It's US law and it protects them personally, not just us.
4Be yourself about itAuthenticity is the actual brand value, not a slogan. If a post feels like a paid ad it underperforms and costs trust they can't buy back.

Strongly recommended: ManyChat on their own account

The creators earning most aren't posting more — they're capturing more of the people who already engaged. ManyChat auto-replies to comments and sends the link in the DM. Free to start, twenty minutes to set up. We walk them through the exact triggers and DM copy that work for this product. If they take one piece of technical advice from the handbook, it's this one.

Talking about the product without creating a problem

They can talk about how it makes them feel. They can't say it treats a medical condition. That's the whole thing, and the gap is wider than people assume.

Brain fog, focus, energy, mornings, how a day goes with it and without it — all fine, and it converts better than anything clinical anyway. What we steer away from is language that sounds like medicine: cures, treats, heals, prevents, fixes a named condition. Not because anyone's coming after them for a passing comment, but because that language quietly makes the post worse — it stops sounding like them and starts sounding like a supplement ad.

Unsure about a sentence? Send it to the creator inbox. Same-day answer, and nobody gets made to feel silly for asking.

Say this, not that

Instead ofTry
"Cures my brain fog""Cuts through the fog for me"
"Treats my ADHD""On days my focus is everywhere, this is what I reach for"
"Fixes anxiety""Part of how I keep myself steady"
"Boosts your immune system""Part of my daily routine"
"Clinically proven to…""Here's what the research says about lion's mane" (+ link)
"Detoxes your body""How I start my mornings clean"
"Will give you energy for hours""I don't get the 2pm crash with this"
"Heals your gut""Sits easier on my stomach than regular coffee"

The pattern: move the claim from the product does X to here's what happens for me. Same message, their voice, no exposure.

Rules of the voice

Always

  • Lead with something true and specific. A number, an observation, a moment. Not "In today's fast-paced world."
  • Short paragraphs. One to three sentences.
  • Speak to "you." Singular. One reader.
  • Cite the science when you invoke it. A link beats an adjective.
  • Name real things. The 8 oz coffee, the oxblood vessel, Ethiopia, 6am.
  • One clear next thing at the end.

Never

  • Buzzwords. Game-changing, revolutionary, unlock, elevate, curated, journey.
  • "Imagine if you…" Tell them what's real.
  • Event recaps as the body. A hike photo belongs in a Practice issue about walking.
  • Treatment claims. Same rules as affiliates.
  • More than one call to action.
  • Exclamation marks. Quiet authority doesn't shout.

How three a week actually happens

SM-AFF-07

AI UGC — show them what good looks like.

The hardest part of running a creator program isn't recruiting. It's that a new affiliate opens the app, points the camera at a coffee bag, and has no idea what we want. Telling them "be authentic" is true and completely useless on its own. Showing them fifteen examples closes that gap in thirty seconds.

Producing fifteen real examples with real people costs thousands and takes weeks. Producing them with AI costs almost nothing and takes an afternoon — which means we can make them specific to each lane: one set for the fitness creator, one for the recipe creator, one for the woman filming her first hour before work.

The decision before anything publishes

World Bible Edition 02 says generated imagery is internal art direction unless a publishable tier is agreed with Keys, and that synthetic humans presented as real people should not publish. The two risks worth naming

  • The endorsement problem. A generated person presented as a customer describing their experience is a testimonial from someone who doesn't exist. Product scenes and lifestyle b-roll aren't endorsements; a face saying "this changed my mornings" is.
  • The brand problem, which is bigger. Authenticity is the value we ask every affiliate to lead with. If our own feed is synthetic people performing sincerity, that's the story if anyone notices — and the audience most likely to notice is the one we're trying to win.

Recommendation: build Track A immediately — most of the value, none of the exposure. Bring Track B to Keys as a specific proposal with examples in hand, not an abstract question. If he wants it: generated people in lifestyle scenes, not testimonials, no first-person claims from any generated face, an AI-assisted label where it could read as real, and real people carrying every testimonial.

Production rules and where the library gets used

  • Never let a model draw the wordmark. Generate without packaging text, composite the real mark in post. A hallucinated logo is the fastest way to look fake.
  • One committed light source. Warm practicals for archive scenes, natural daylight for living scenes.
  • Shadows lean warm brown and burgundy, never grey.
  • One intentional idea per frame. Clutter reads as AI.
  • Casting leads with Black women, 25–45 — composed, put-together, natural skin texture, real hair, relaxed authority rather than excitement.
  • Every asset tagged internal reference or approved for publication, in the filename. No exceptions.

Three identity rules that apply to everyone here

The wordmark is supplied artwork. Place it — never retype it in a font however close, never stretch, outline, rotate or recolour it, and never use a screenshot of it. Anyone who needs the mark gets the folder.

Minimum size and space. 160 pixels wide on screen, with clear space of at least half the mark's height on every side.

Nothing physical ships yet. The production vector is still pending, so these files are for digital, decks, social and web only — not print, packaging, foil, engraving, embroidery or signage.
